## Why Most PDF to Excel Converters Destroy Table Formatting

To understand why tables get scrambled during conversion, it is essential to look at how PDF files store data under the hood.

Under the official **ISO 32000** document exchange standard, a PDF file has no native concept of a table, rows, columns, or cells. Unlike HTML (which uses `<table>`, `<tr>`, and `<td>` tags) or spreadsheets, a PDF simply places isolated text operators at fixed 2D Cartesian coordinate points `(X, Y)` across a canvas.

When typical online converters attempt to convert this into an Excel sheet, they suffer from three major design flaws:

1. **Point-Coordinate Splitting (Phantom Columns)**: If the word *"City"* in the header starts at $X = 265$ and the word *"Mumbai"* in the data row starts at $X = 255$, primitive converters assume they belong to two completely different columns. This generates dozens of empty ghost columns filled with dashes.
2. **Fragmented Multi-Line Rows**: In financial statements, item descriptions and transaction memos often wrap across two or three lines, while adjacent monetary columns (Amounts, Balances) only occupy a single baseline. Low-tier converters split the wrapped description onto new rows, destroying the one-to-one accounting relationship.
3. **Static Text Numbers Instead of Real Numbers**: Numbers formatted with commas, negative parentheses `(1,250.00)`, or currency symbols (`$`, `₹`, `€`, `£`) are frequently written to Excel as raw text strings. As a result, accounting formulas cannot calculate them without manual cleanup.

The solution is using an advanced geometric extraction engine that performs horizontal word assembly, adaptive column track projection, and multi-line row stitching.
